## CHAPTER 25: Venous Malformations
**Pages: 681-692**

### Overview Section
**Topic:** Classification of Vascular Anomalies
**Pages:** 681-683.e1
- **Key Content:**
  - Vascular malformations are errors in vasculogenesis
  - Occur in ~1.5% of population; >90% present at birth
  - ISSVA Classification System (2014) - separates tumors from vascular malformations
  - Definition: Malformed or dysplastic embryologic spaces with normal endothelial cell turnover
  - All vascular malformations can be grouped into three types: **Low Flow, High Flow, and Combined**

### Key Distinction: Hemangiomas vs AVMs
**Pages:** 681.e1-683
- **Important Clarification:**
  - Hemangiomas are NOT arteriovenous malformations (AVMs), and vice versa
  - Infantile hemangiomas are benign vascular tumors (childhood-only birthmarks)
  - AVMs demonstrate significant venous shunting with low-resistance arterial inflow
  - Hemangiomas show minimal shunting in outflow vessels

## 1. LOW-FLOW VASCULAR MALFORMATIONS

### 1.1 Venous Malformations (Low-Flow)
**Pages:** 684-685

#### Definition & Characteristics:
- **Description:** Number of tortuous vascular channels
- **Flow:** Low-flow lesions
- **Onset:** Present at birth, grow with child
- **Clinical Presentation:**
  - Bluish-purple skin discoloration (superficial)
  - Local swelling and pain (deep)
  - Extremity varicosities
  - Soft, easily compressible on exam
  - Engorgement in dependent position

#### Anatomical Classification:
1. **Truncal Lesions:**
   - Occur in differentiated, later-stage vascular structures
   - Impressive cutaneous manifestation
   - Limb swelling and varicosities
   - Better localized treatment outcomes

2. **Extratruncal Lesions:**
   - Remnants of primitive vessels from early development
   - Usually dysplastic and diffusely infiltrative
   - Involve deep soft tissue structures
   - Constant pain common

3. **Diffuse Malformations:**
   - Multiple areas/regions involvement
   - Usually part of syndrome (Klippel-Trenaunay syndrome)

#### MRI Characteristics (Diagnosis):
- T1 isointense to muscle
- T2 hyperintense
- Late gadolinium enhancement

#### Treatment Approach:
- **Pages:** 687-692
- Sclerotherapy (ethanol, foam sclerotherapy)
- Direct puncture or transcatheter approach
- Multiple sessions (2-4 treatments typical)
- Spacing: 3-4 months between treatments
- Endpoint: Pain symptom resolution
- Asymptomatic lesions typically not treated

## 2. HIGH-FLOW VASCULAR MALFORMATIONS

### 2.1 Arteriovenous Fistulas (AVF)
**Pages:** 685-686

#### Definition:
- **Direct abnormal connection between artery and vein**
- High-flow lesions
- Can be congenital or acquired

#### Etiology:
- **Congenital:** Present from birth
- **Acquired:** 
  - Surgical complications
  - Penetrating trauma
  - Erosion by adjacent disease processes

#### Pathophysiology:
- **Steal Phenomenon:** Low-resistance fistula causes "parasitic circulation"
- Flow follows path of least resistance
- Increased flow in both inflow artery and outflow vein
- Can cause reversal of arterial flow in distal segment
- Results in decreased arterial pressure in distal capillary beds
- **Clinical Consequence:** Tissue ischemia and pain

#### Treatment:
- Usually endovascular approach
- Location-dependent treatment strategy

---

### 2.2 Arteriovenous Malformations (AVMs)
**Pages:** 685-686

#### Definition:
- **Abnormal connections between artery and vein with tortuous dysplastic vessels (NIDUS)**
- High-flow lesions
- Usually asymptomatic (can be symptomatic)

#### Anatomical Components:
1. **Feeding Artery/Arteries:** Supply the malformation
2. **Nidus:** Central tortuous segment of dysplastic vessels
   - Acts as the shunt
   - Multiple arteriolar-venular communications
   - High-resistance area requiring treatment

3. **Draining Vein/Veins:** Outflow vessel(s)
   - Often enlarged
   - Pressurized from arterial shunting

#### Clinical Presentation (if symptomatic):
- Heart failure
- Neuropathy
- Pain
- Bleeding

#### Treatment Approach:
- **Pages:** 685-686
- Surgical resection (small, superficial AVMs only)
- Endovascular embolotherapy (primary mode for most cases)
- Superselective catheter technique
- Liquid embolic agents
- Treatment depends on type (see Yakes Classification below)

## 3. YAKES ARTERIOVENOUS MALFORMATION CLASSIFICATION
**Pages:** 685-686**

### Classification System Overview:
**Purpose:** Characterize AVMs based on angioarchitecture to suggest treatment strategies
**Developer:** Yakes WF (2015)

### Type I AVM
**Subtype:** Simple Direct Arteriovenous Fistula
**Characteristics:**
- Direct artery-to-vein connection
- No nidus present
- Simplest form

**Treatment Strategy:**
- Mechanical device occlusion
- Coils
- Plugs
- Direct closure methods

---

### Type IIa AVM
**Subtype:** Multiple Inflow, Traditional Central Nidus
**Characteristics:**
- Multiple inflow arteries
- Traditional central nidus
- Direct artery-to-arteriolar communication
- Vein-to-venule communication
- Low-complexity nidus

**Treatment Strategy:**
- Focus on sclerosing the nidus
- Via arterial inflow vessel, OR
- Direct injection into nidus
- Objective: Nidus obliteration

---

### Type IIb AVM
**Subtype:** Multiple Inflow with Enlarged Draining Vein
**Characteristics:**
- Multiple inflow arteries
- Characteristic nidus
- Nidus shunts into ENLARGED draining vein
- Increased venous pressure from shunting

**Treatment Strategy:**
- Focus on sclerosing the nidus
- Via arterial inflow vessel, OR
- Direct injection approach
- Key: Address high-flow draining vein
- May require combined approach

---

### Type IIIa AVM
**Subtype:** Central Vein as Nidus - Single Outflow
**Characteristics:**
- Multiple artery-to-arteriole communications
- Communicate into ENLARGED CENTRAL VEIN
- **Vein wall itself serves as the nidus** (KEY FEATURE)
- Single draining vein
- High-flow pattern

**Treatment Strategy:**
- Transarterial embolization of nidus
- PLUS
- Direct puncture of enlarged central vein
- Occlusion of venous outflow vessel
- Dual-phase approach critical

---

### Type IIIb AVM
**Subtype:** Central Vein as Nidus - Multiple Outflows
**Characteristics:**
- Multiple artery-to-arteriole communications
- Communicate into ENLARGED CENTRAL VEIN
- **Multiple draining veins** (often dilated)
- Complex high-flow pattern
- Most complex venous drainage scenario

**Treatment Strategy:**
- Transarterial embolization of arterial inflow
- PLUS
- Direct puncture and occlusion of dominant venous outflow vessel
- Occlusion of additional outflow veins with coils/plugs
- Multi-staged approach often needed

**Example Case:** Pages 689-691
- 22-year-old male, left thigh painful venous malformation (Yakes IIIa)
- Treatment: Direct injection of nidus vessels + closure of dominant outflow vein

---

### Type IV AVM
**Subtype:** Diffuse Infiltrative Pattern
**Characteristics:**
- Multiple small arteriolar-to-venule communications
- Diffusely infiltrates tissues
- No discrete nidus
- Widely distributed shunting pattern
- Most challenging to treat

**Treatment Strategy:**
- Superselective ethanol (50%) injection
- Via direct injection approach
- Via transcatheter approach
- Focus on chemical sclerosis of diffuse lesion
- May require multiple staged procedures

## 5. TREATMENT TECHNIQUES AND CONSIDERATIONS

### Access and Preparation
**Pages:** 687-689

**Access Methods:**
1. **Direct Puncture:**
   - Ultrasound-guided
   - Fluoroscopic guidance
   - Biplane fluoroscopy when available

2. **Transcatheter Approach:**
   - Through arterial inflow
   - For specific AVM types

**Imaging Techniques:**
- Small angiocatheter for direct puncture venography (Fig. 25.5)
- Allows detailed nidus visualization
- Used to plan treatment volumes

### Sclerotherapy Agents
**Pages:** 687-691

#### Ethyl Alcohol (Dehydrated)
**Dosing Guidelines:**
- **Absolute maximum:** 1 mL per kg
- **Standard maximum:** 0.5 mL per kg per procedure
- **Working dose limit:** 0.25 mg per kg for entire case
- **Injection volume limit:** 0.1 mL per kg per injection
- **Interval between injections:** ≥5 minutes

**Administration Technique:**
- **Negative Contrast Technique:**
  - Opacify malformation with contrast first
  - Inject ethanol to replace contrast
  - Live fluoroscopic visualization of alcohol
  - Allows real-time control

**Safety Precautions:**
- General anesthesia mandatory
- Risk of cardiovascular collapse
- Potential for transmural necrosis and spread

#### Sodium Tetradecyl Sulfate (STS)
**Preparation:**
- Can be injected as liquid
- Foam preparation (3-4% STS from 3 mL syringe)
- Foam more viscous and easier to control

**Application Methods:**
- Transcatheter approach
- Direct puncture approach
- Negative contrast technique (Figs. 25.8-25.10)

### Venous Drainage Assessment
**Pages:** 691

**CRITICAL SAFETY FACTOR:**

**Critical Consideration:**
- Evaluate venous drainage pattern BEFORE treatment
- **High-Risk Scenario:** Large-diameter connections to deep venous system
- **Goal:** Prevent liquid embolics from entering central veins
- **Implication:** Defines treatment approach and complication risk

**Drainage Classification:**
1. **No/Limited Drainage:** 
   - Higher treatment effectiveness
   - Lower complication risk
   - Easier control

2. **Normal Draining Vessels:**
   - Moderate risk
   - Standard approach

3. **Drainage into Dysplastic Veins/Venous Ectasia:**
   - Most problematic to treat
   - Higher complication risk
   - Requires careful control

### Treatment Outcomes
**Pages:** 691-692

**Success Rates:**
- Burrows et al. series: 75-90% good results with serial alcohol administration

**Clinical Course:**
- Most lesions require 2-4 treatment sessions
- Spacing: 3-4 months between procedures
- Total endpoint: Pain symptom resolution
- Asymptomatic lesions typically not treated
- Cosmetic issues (face/neck) may warrant treatment

**Post-Procedure Management:**
- General anesthesia for procedure
- 24-hour observation
- Intravenous: Ketorolac, dexamethasone, antibiotics
- Discharge medications:
  - Methylprednisolone dose pack
  - Ibuprofen
  - Oral antibiotics
- Chronic pain may persist despite technical success

## 6. KEY DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES

### Hemangioma vs AVM Distinction
**Pages:** 682-683

| Feature | Hemangioma | AVM |
|---------|-----------|-----|
| **Age of Presentation** | Childhood only | Any age (congenital or acquired) |
| **Tumor Type** | Benign tumor with high endothelial turnover | Vascular malformation, not tumor |
| **Arterial Pattern** | Central arterial pedicle | Multiple feeding arteries (usually) |
| **Venous Shunting** | Minimal to none | Significant shunting |
| **Venous Outflow** | Normal appearance | Arterialized, pulsatile, enlarged |
| **Flow Pattern** | Typical tumor vascularity | Low-resistance shunt pattern |
| **Growth** | Proliferative then involutional phase | Grows with body, no involution |
| **Cell Markers** | GLUT-1, merosin, Lewis Y (placental markers) | Normal endothelial markers |

**Clinical Pearl:** Hemangiomas in adults are NOT hemangiomas - if vascular lesion wasn't present in childhood, it's not a hemangioma.

## 7. INTERNATIONAL CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM

### ISSVA Classification (2014)
**Pages:** 682

**Table 25.1: Abbreviated ISSVA Classification**

#### Vascular Tumors (Benign to Malignant):
- Benign: Infantile hemangioma, Congenital hemangioma, Tufted hemangioma, etc.
- Locally Aggressive: Kaposiform hemangioendothelioma, Retiform, PILA
- Malignant: Angiosarcoma, Epithelioid hemangioendothelioma

#### Vascular Malformations (Simple to Combined):
**Simple Malformations:**
- Capillary malformation (CM)
- Lymphatic malformation (LM)
- Venous malformation (VM)
- Arteriovenous malformation (AVM)
- Arteriovenous fistula

**Combined Malformations:**
- Capillary-venous (CVM)
- Capillary-lymphatic (CLM)
- Lymphatic-venous (LVM)
- Capillary-arteriovenous (CAVM)
- Cerebral-lymphatic-arteriovenous (CLAVM)

## 10. SEVERITY ASSESSMENT FOR VENOUS DISEASE

### CEAP Classification System
**Pages:** 697-698 (Chapter 26)
**Relevance to AVM/Shunts:** Secondary venous disease from AVF

**Etiologic Classification:**
- **Ec:** Congenital (includes congenital AVMs)
- **Es:** Secondary (includes acquired AVF)
- **Ep:** Primary
- **En:** No venous cause identified

**Pathophysiologic Classification:**
- **Pr:** Reflux
- **Po:** Obstruction
- **Pr,o:** Reflux and obstruction
- **Pn:** No venous pathophysiology identifiable

## 11. DIAGNOSTIC AND TREATMENT ALGORITHMS

### Diagnostic Workup for Suspected Shunt/AVM:
1. **Clinical Examination:**
   - Soft, compressible lesion?
   - High-flow vs low-flow appearance?
   - Pulsatility?
   - Color/blanching?

2. **Imaging - Ultrasound Doppler:**
   - Assess flow velocity
   - Determine high-flow vs low-flow pattern
   - Identify feeding artery and draining vein

3. **Imaging - MRI:**
   - T1/T2/Post-gadolinium characteristics
   - Assess extent and adjacent structure involvement
   - Plan direct access

4. **Angiography/Venography:**
   - Define exact angioarchitecture
   - Classify by Yakes system
   - Determine treatment approach

### Treatment Decision Algorithm:

**Step 1: Identify Lesion Type**
- AVM Type (I-IV) vs simple AVF
- Assess complexity

**Step 2: Symptom Assessment**
- Symptomatic (pain, ischemia, cosmetic) vs asymptomatic
- Only treat if symptomatic or significant cosmetic issue

**Step 3: Select Approach Based on Yakes Type:**
- **Type I:** Mechanical devices
- **Type IIa/IIb:** Sclerotherapy (nidus focus)
- **Type IIIa:** Arterial + venous occlusion
- **Type IIIb:** Multi-vessel occlusion strategy
- **Type IV:** Superselective ethanol injection

**Step 4: Assess Venous Drainage**
- Low risk (minimal connections): Proceed with embolics
- High risk (central vein connections): Careful planning, may need multi-stage approach

## 12. KEY LEARNING POINTS FOR RAG TRAINING

### Critical Distinctions for Classification:

1. **Shunt vs Malformation:**
   - **Fistula (Type I):** Direct A-V connection only
   - **AVM:** A-V connection WITH dysplastic nidus between them

2. **Nidus Recognition:**
   - Central dysplastic vessel network
   - Can be traditional central nidus (Types IIa, IIb)
   - Can be in vein wall itself (Types IIIa, IIIb)
   - Can be diffusely infiltrative (Type IV)

3. **Inflow-Outflow Assessment:**
   - **Inflow:** Number of feeding arteries (single vs multiple)
   - **Nidus:** Characteristics and location
   - **Outflow:** Single vs multiple draining veins, size, dysplasia

4. **Clinical Reasoning:**
   - High-flow lesions = arterial input drives symptoms
   - Steal phenomenon indicates low-resistance shunt
   - Venous enlargement indicates pressurized flow
   - Pain/ischemia indicates significant hemodynamic steal

5. **Treatment Reasoning:**
   - **Type-specific approaches:** Each Yakes type requires different strategy
   - **Sclerotherapy agents:** Ethanol more aggressive, STS safer but less effective
   - **Access methods:** Direct vs transcatheter based on anatomy
   - **Multi-stage approach:** Most require 2-4 sessions for symptom resolution
