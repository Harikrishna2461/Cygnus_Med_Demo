## CHAPTER 19: Venous Malformations
**Pages: 433-441**

### Overview Section
**Topic:** Classification of Vascular Anomalies and Pathophysiology
**Pages:** 433-437

- **Key Content:**
  - Vascular malformations are errors in vasculogenesis
  - Occur in ~1.5% of population; >90% present at birth
  - ISSVA Classification System (1996 adoption) - separates tumors from vascular malformations
  - Definition: Malformed or dysplastic embryologic spaces with normal endothelial cell turnover
  - All vascular malformations grouped into three types: **Low Flow, High Flow, and Combined**

### Key Distinction: Hemangiomas vs AVMs
**Pages:** 433-437
- **Important Clarification:**
  - Hemangiomas are NOT arteriovenous malformations (AVMs), and vice versa
  - Infantile hemangiomas are benign vascular tumors (childhood-only birthmarks)
  - AVMs demonstrate significant venous shunting with low-resistance arterial inflow
  - Hemangiomas show minimal shunting in outflow vessels
  - Venous enlargement common in AVMs from pressurized shunted arterial flow into nidus

## 1. LOW-FLOW VASCULAR MALFORMATIONS

### 1.1 Venous Malformations (Low-Flow)
**Pages:** 434-435

#### Definition & Characteristics:
- **Description:** Number of tortuous vascular channels
- **Flow:** Low-flow lesions
- **Onset:** Present at birth, grow with child
- **Clinical Presentation:**
  - Bluish-purple skin discoloration (superficial)
  - Local swelling and pain (deep subcutaneous/intramuscular)
  - Extremity varicosities (especially in deep lesions)
  - Soft, easily compressible on exam
  - Engorgement in dependent position
  - Can be clinically occult if deep in location
  - Usually symptomatic in late childhood/early adolescence

#### Anatomical Classification:
1. **Truncal Lesions:**
   - Occur in differentiated, later-stage vascular structures
   - Impressive cutaneous manifestation
   - Limb swelling and varicosities
   - Better localized treatment outcomes

2. **Extratruncal Lesions:**
   - Remnants of primitive vessels from early development
   - Usually dysplastic and diffusely infiltrative
   - Involve deep soft tissue structures
   - Constant pain common
   - More difficult to treat

3. **Diffuse Malformations:**
   - Multiple areas/regions involvement
   - Usually part of syndrome (Klippel-Trenaunay syndrome)

#### MRI Characteristics (Diagnosis):
- T1 isointense to muscle
- T2 hyperintense
- Late gadolinium enhancement
- Shows extent and guides treatment planning

#### Clinical Diagnosis:
- Usually by clinical examination
- MRI for extent evaluation and treatment guidance
- Fig. 19-1: Example of 10-year-old with focal thigh swelling and pain
  - T1, T2, and post-gadolinium imaging patterns
  - T1 isointense and T2 hyperintense signal
  - Late enhancement after gadolinium

#### Treatment Approach:
- **Pages:** 438-440
- Sclerotherapy (ethanol, foam sclerotherapy)
- Direct puncture or transcatheter approach
- Multiple sessions (2-4 treatments typical)
- Spacing: 3-4 months between treatments
- Endpoint: Pain symptom resolution
- Asymptomatic lesions typically not treated
- Cosmetic issues (face/neck) may warrant treatment

### 1.2 Lymphatic Malformations (Low-Flow)
**Pages:** 435-436

#### Definition & Characteristics:
- Abnormalities of lymphatic etiology
- Appear in infancy or early childhood
- >90% present by age 2 years
- >75% located in craniocervical region
- Often fluctuate in size (trauma, inflammation, hemorrhage)

#### Distinguishing Features from Venous Malformations:
- Usually NOT painful (unlike venous malformations)
- Cosmetic concerns drive earlier treatment
- Can cause airway obstruction, speech abnormalities, dysphagia

#### Subtypes and Treatment:
1. **Macrocystic LM:**
   - Appear similar to venous malformations on MRI
   - Minimal peripheral enhancement
   - Often easily accessible for sclerotherapy

2. **Microcystic LM:**
   - Often only observed
   - Treatment limited to surgical debulking
   - Percutaneous management of cutaneous complications
   - Sclerotherapy for bleeding superficial vesicles

#### Terminology Note:
- Outdated terms: lymphangiomas, cystic hygromas
- Should no longer be used in clinical practice

---

### 1.3 Capillary Malformations (Low-Flow)
**Pages:** 436

#### Definition:
- Capillary dilatations
- Ectatic papillary dermal capillaries and postcapillary venules
- Commonly referred to as "port-wine" stains

#### Characteristics:
- Present at birth
- Grow commensurate with child
- No tendency toward involution
- Pink or reddish at birth, darken with age
- Usually solitary but can be associated with other vascular malformations

#### Clinical Associations:
- Midline lower back: may associate with tethered spinal cord
- Facial: may associate with Sturge-Weber syndrome
- Associated with Klippel-Trenaunay syndrome
- Associated with Proteus syndrome

## 2. HIGH-FLOW VASCULAR MALFORMATIONS

### 2.1 Arteriovenous Fistulas (AVF)
**Pages:** 437

#### Definition:
- **Direct abnormal connection between artery and vein**
- High-flow lesions
- Can be congenital or acquired

#### Etiology:
- **Congenital:** Present from birth
- **Acquired:** 
  - Surgical complications (most common)
  - Penetrating trauma
  - Erosion by adjacent disease processes

#### Pathophysiology:
- **Steal Phenomenon:** Low-resistance fistula causes "parasitic circulation"
- Flow follows path of least resistance
- Increased flow in both inflow artery and outflow vein
- Can cause reversal of arterial flow in distal segment
- Results in decreased arterial pressure in distal capillary beds
- **Clinical Consequences:** Tissue ischemia and pain

#### Treatment:
- Usually endovascular approach
- Location-dependent treatment strategy

---

### 2.2 Arteriovenous Malformations (AVMs)
**Pages:** 437

#### Definition:
- **Abnormal connections between artery and vein with tortuous dysplastic vessels (NIDUS)**
- High-flow lesions
- Usually asymptomatic (can be symptomatic)

#### Anatomical Components:
1. **Feeding Artery/Arteries:** Supply the malformation
2. **Nidus:** Tortuous segment of dysplastic vessels
   - Acts as the shunt
   - Multiple arteriolar-venular communications
   - High-resistance area requiring treatment
   - Can vary in size and complexity

3. **Draining Vein/Veins:** Outflow vessel(s)
   - Often enlarged
   - Pressurized from arterial shunting

#### Clinical Presentation (if symptomatic):
- Heart failure
- Neuropathy
- Pain
- Bleeding

#### Treatment Approach:
- **Pages:** 437
- Surgical resection (small, superficial AVMs only)
  - Occasionally used
  - Limited to specific presentations

- Endovascular embolotherapy (PRIMARY MODE for most cases):
  - Emerged as primary treatment mode
  - Superselective catheter technique
  - Liquid embolic agents
  - Treatment depends on anatomy and characteristics

## 3. ARTERIOVENOUS FISTULA (AVF) AND SECONDARY VENOUS DISEASE
**Pages (Chapter 3, Venous Pathophysiology): 2480-2520**

### Clinical Significance of AVF in Venous Disease

#### Historical Overview:
- AVFs as cause of secondary chronic venous disease (CVD) reduced compared to postthrombotic CVD
- Most frequently created during endovascular procedures:
  - Inadvertent puncture of both common femoral vessels
  - Can occur with penetrating or blunt traumatic injuries

#### Pathophysiologic Progression of AVF-Induced Venous Disease:

**Phase 1 - Acute Changes:**
- Lower resistance in distal artery
- Pulsatile flow in vein
- Dilatation of draining veins

**Phase 2 - Intermediate Changes:**
- Venous hypertension supervenes
- Distension of veins
- Limb edema develops
- NO reflux at this stage

**Phase 3 - Chronic Changes:**
- Venous valves unable to appose
- Development of reflux
- Continuous venous hypertension
- Valve atrophy
- "Arterialization" of venous wall in long term

#### Clinical Observations:
- AVF-induced CVD in humans is RARE and requires long course to develop
- Clinical impairment when it occurs is frequently devastating
- Example case (Fig. 3-8): Gunshot wound to left groin
  - Fistula between deep femoral artery and common femoral vein
  - High velocities with low resistance pattern
  - Turbulent flow in CFV proximal to fistula
  - CFV dilation (2.8 cm) due to local high pressure
  - SFJ dilatation and GSV dilatation with continuous reflux

#### Diagnostic Imaging Findings for AVF:
- **Duplex Ultrasound:**
  - High velocities in fistula tract
  - Low resistance waveform pattern
  - Turbulent flow distal to fistula
  - Dilated draining vein

- **Spectral Doppler:**
  - Loss of normal pulsatile venous waveform
  - Continuous flow pattern in draining vein
  - Arterialized waveform

## 4. TREATMENT TECHNIQUES AND CONSIDERATIONS

### Access and Preparation
**Pages:** 438-440

**Access Methods:**
1. **Direct Puncture:**
   - Ultrasound-guided
   - Fluoroscopic guidance
   - Biplane fluoroscopy when available

2. **Transcatheter Approach:**
   - Through arterial inflow
   - For specific malformation types

**Imaging Techniques:**
- Ultrasound Doppler examination (Fig. 19-2)
  - Heterogeneous lesion with low flow pattern
  - No increased flow for venous malformations
  
- MRI assessment (Fig. 19-3)
  - Coronal fat-saturated T1-weighted
  - Post-gadolinium enhancement of dysplastic draining vein
  
- Fluoroscopic venography (Fig. 19-4, 19-5)
  - Initial venogram with dilute contrast
  - Direct puncture venography using small angiocatheter

**Positioning and Setup:**
- Proper positioning on angiographic table critical for successful access
- Proper anesthesia monitoring
- All alcohol procedures under general anesthesia

**Optional Adjuncts:**
- Tourniquet for control (calibrated cuff)
- When used: do not exceed diastolic BP
- Minimal pressures typically 20-40 mm Hg range

### Sclerotherapy Agents and Dosing
**Pages:** 438-440

#### Ethyl Alcohol (Dehydrated)
**Dosing Guidelines:**
- **Absolute maximum:** 1 mL/kg
- **Standard maximum:** 0.5 mL/kg per procedure
- **Working dose limit:** 0.25 mg/kg for entire case
- **Injection volume limit:** 0.1 mL/kg per injection
- **Interval between injections:** ≥5 minutes

**Administration Technique - Negative Contrast:**
- Opacify malformation with contrast first
- Inject ethanol to replace contrast
- Live fluoroscopic visualization of alcohol
- Allows real-time control and monitoring

**Safety Precautions:**
- General anesthesia mandatory
- Risk of cardiovascular collapse possible
- Potential for transmural necrosis and spread to surrounding tissues
- Safe completion in many patients WITHOUT pulmonary arterial catheter monitoring (using adherence to dosing recommendations)

**Previous Technique (Older Method):**
- Dehydrated ethanol opacified with ethiodized oil (Ethiodol)
- Used to increase visualization of administered alcohol under fluoroscopy
- Largely replaced by negative contrast technique

**Figure References:**
- Fig. 19-5: Direct puncture venography using small angiocatheter
- Fig. 19-6: Posttreatment scout film demonstrating two separate treatment punctures

### Treatment Course and Follow-Up
**Pages:** 439-440

#### Number of Sessions:
- Some lesions treated in single session (occasionally)
- Most require 2-4 treatment sessions
- Reach clinical endpoint of pain symptom resolution

#### Treatment Frequency:
- Spacing: 3-4 months between procedures
- Allow time for postprocedural edema to resolve

#### Asymptomatic Lesion Management:
- Do not treat asymptomatic lesions routinely
- Exception: Significant cosmetic issue (face/neck lesions)

#### Post-Procedure Management:
**Immediate (24 hours):**
- Intravenous ketorolac
- Intravenous dexamethasone
- Intravenous antibiotic coverage

**Discharge (after overnight observation):**
- Methylprednisolone dose pack
- Ibuprofen therapy
- Oral antibiotics

#### Clinical Outcomes:
- Burrows et al. series: 75-90% good results with serial alcohol administration
- Many patients have chronic pain symptoms
- Clinical improvement may lag significantly behind technically successful treatment

## 5. VENOUS DRAINAGE ASSESSMENT - CRITICAL FACTOR
**Pages:** 440

### Importance in Treatment Planning:

**Drainage Assessment is ESSENTIAL for:**
- Treatment success prediction
- Complication risk stratification
- Sclerosant volume control
- Overall management strategy

### Drainage Pattern Classification:

**1. No or Limited Venous Drainage:**
- Higher treatment effectiveness
- Lower complication risk
- Safer embolic agent use
- Easier technical control

**2. Normal Draining Vessels:**
- Moderate risk category
- Standard treatment approach

**3. Drainage into Dysplastic Veins or Venous Ectasia:**
- **Most problematic to treat**
- Highest complication risk
- Requires very careful control
- May need alternative approaches

### Critical Safety Consideration:
**Large-Diameter Connections to Deep Venous System:**
- Malformations may have multiple large connections to deep veins
- Essential to control injection carefully
- Prevent liquid embolics from entering central veins
- Risk of systemic embolization if not controlled

## 6. KEY DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES

### Hemangioma vs AVM Distinction
**Pages:** 433-437

| Feature | Hemangioma | AVM |
|---------|-----------|-----|
| **Age of Presentation** | Childhood only | Any age (congenital or acquired) |
| **Tumor Type** | Benign tumor with high endothelial turnover | Vascular malformation, NOT tumor |
| **Cell Markers** | GLUT-1, merosin, Lewis Y (placental markers) | Normal endothelial markers |
| **Arterial Pattern** | Central arterial pedicle | Variable inflow (single to multiple arteries) |
| **Venous Shunting** | Minimal to none | Significant shunting |
| **Venous Outflow** | Normal appearance | Arterialized, pulsatile, enlarged |
| **Flow Pattern** | Typical tumor vascularity | Low-resistance shunt pattern |
| **Growth** | Proliferative then involutional phase | Grows with body, no involution |
| **Nidus** | Absent | Present (dysplastic vessel network) |

**Clinical Pearl:** Hemangiomas in adults are NOT hemangiomas - if vascular lesion wasn't present in childhood, it's not a hemangioma.

## 7. INTERNATIONAL CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM

### ISSVA Classification (1996 Adoption)
**Pages:** 433

**Characteristics:**
- Clearly separates tumors (e.g., hemangioma) from vascular malformations
- Organized classification system
- Based on Mullikan, Glowacki, and colleagues (1992 original presentation)

**Key Distinction from Earlier Classifications:**
- Clear demarcation between benign vascular tumors and malformations
- Provides framework for consistent terminology and communication

## 8. SECONDARY VENOUS DISEASE FROM AVF
**Chapter 3: Venous Pathophysiology, Pages: 2480-2520**

### Epidemiology:
- Incidence as cause of secondary CVD is reduced (compared to postthrombotic CVD)
- Rare in human population to develop significant CVD from AVF
- Requires long time course to manifest clinically
- When it does occur, clinical impairment is frequently devastating

### Etiologic Factors:
- **Most Common Source:** Inadvertent puncture of both CFV and DFA during procedures
- **Traumatic Sources:**
  - Penetrating injuries (gunshot wounds, stab wounds)
  - Blunt trauma

### Animal Model Studies:
- AVF creation has been used in animal models to study CVD pathophysiology
- Helps explain findings of chronic venous disease development

### Hemodynamic Changes:
1. **Initial:** Lower resistance in distal artery segment
2. **Initial:** Pulsatile flow appears in vein
3. **Progressive:** Venous hypertension develops
4. **Progressive:** Vein distension and limb edema
5. **Chronic:** Valve incompetence develops
6. **Chronic:** Reflux develops
7. **Long-term:** Arterialization of venous wall

### Clinical Example (Fig. 3-8):
**Case:** Gunshot wound, left groin
- **Pathology:** Fistula between deep femoral artery and common femoral vein
- **Duplex Findings:**
  - High velocities
  - Low resistance pattern
  - Turbulent flow in CFV proximal to fistula
  - CFV dilation to 2.8 cm
  - SFJ dilatation
  - GSV dilatation
  - Continuous reflux flow
- **Clinical Presentation:**
  - Limb swelling
  - Pain
  - Discoloration

## 9. TREATMENT PHILOSOPHY AND APPROACH

### General Principles:
1. **Symptomatic lesions take priority** for treatment
2. **Low-flow malformations** respond better to sclerotherapy
3. **High-flow malformations** require more complex approaches
4. **Venous drainage assessment** guides treatment strategy
5. **Multi-session approach** often necessary for optimal results

### Indications for Treatment:
- **Pain relief** - primary endpoint
- **Ischemia prevention** (steal phenomenon management)
- **Cosmetic concerns** (face/neck lesions)
- **Prevention of complications** (bleeding, infection)

### Contraindications/Cautions:
- **Asymptomatic lesions** - generally observation
- **Lesions with extensive deep venous connections** - increased risk
- **Central nervous system lesions** - specialized approach needed

## 10. DIAGNOSTIC AND TREATMENT ALGORITHMS

### Diagnostic Workup for Suspected Shunt/AVM:

**Step 1: Clinical Examination**
- Soft, compressible lesion?
- Palpable pulsatility?
- Color characteristics?
- Blanching response?
- Dependent position engorgement?

**Step 2: Duplex Ultrasound**
- Assess flow velocity
- Determine high-flow vs low-flow pattern
- Identify feeding artery/arteries
- Identify draining vein/veins
- Assess waveform patterns

**Step 3: MRI Study**
- T1/T2/Post-gadolinium characteristics
- Assess extent and distribution
- Evaluate adjacent structure involvement
- Plan direct access approach

**Step 4: Angiography/Venography**
- Define exact anatomy
- Assess nidus characteristics
- Determine venous drainage pattern
- Plan treatment approach
- Evaluate for complications (thrombosis, steal)

### Treatment Decision Algorithm:

**Step 1: Lesion Characterization**
- AVF vs AVM vs other malformation?
- Size and location assessment
- Complexity evaluation

**Step 2: Symptom Assessment**
- Symptomatic vs asymptomatic
- Type of symptoms (pain, ischemia, cosmetic, bleeding)
- Impact on function and quality of life

**Step 3: Venous Drainage Evaluation**
- Low-risk drainage pattern: Proceed with standard approach
- High-risk drainage (central connections): Careful planning needed
- Dysplastic/ectasia drainage: Most challenging, may need modifications

**Step 4: Select Treatment Based on Type:**
- **AVF (Simple):** Mechanical device occlusion
- **Low-flow VM:** Sclerotherapy (ethanol or STS)
- **High-flow AVM:** Embolotherapy or combination approach
- **Location-dependent:** Regional approach variations

**Step 5: Treatment Execution**
- Access method selection (direct vs transcatheter)
- Sclerosant agent and dosing
- Imaging guidance optimization
- Complication monitoring

## 12. KEY LEARNING POINTS FOR RAG TRAINING

### Critical Elements for Classification:

1. **Fundamental Distinction:**
   - Fistula: Direct A-V connection only (no nidus)
   - Malformation: A-V connection with dysplastic nidus between them

2. **Flow Characteristics:**
   - **Low-flow lesions:** 
     - Venous malformations (tortuous channels)
     - Respond well to sclerotherapy
   - **High-flow lesions:**
     - AVF and AVM
     - Steal phenomenon common
     - Require more aggressive intervention

3. **Pathophysiology Recognition:**
   - Steal phenomenon indicates significant hemodynamic shunt
   - Venous enlargement indicates pressurized arterial input
   - Arterialized venous waveform confirms high-flow pattern
   - Pain/ischemia correlates with steal severity

4. **Venous Drainage Assessment:**
   - **Critical for treatment success**
   - Limited drainage = safer, more effective treatment
   - Central venous connections = highest risk category
   - Dysplastic drainage = most challenging to treat

5. **Treatment Decision Factors:**
   - Symptom vs asymptomatic status
   - Lesion size and location
   - Venous drainage pattern
   - Patient age and health status
   - Cosmetic considerations

6. **Complications to Monitor:**
   - Transmural necrosis from ethanol
   - Systemic embolization from liquid agents
   - Cardiovascular collapse (general anesthesia risk)
   - Recurrence without adequate nidus treatment
   - Chronic pain despite technical success

## ADDITIONAL CLINICAL NOTES FOR PRACTITIONERS

### When Reviewing Endovascular Procedure Clips:

1. **Identify Flow Pattern:**
   - Look for pulsatile flow in what should be venous system
   - Assess for steal phenomenon signs
   - Evaluate waveform characteristics

2. **Assess Complexity:**
   - Single direct connection = Simple AVF
   - Nidus present = AVM (complexity varies)
   - Multiple draining vessels = Complex pattern

3. **Evaluate Safety Factors:**
   - Proximity to central veins?
   - Evidence of significant steal?
   - Associated thrombosis?

4. **Plan Management:**
   - Symptomatic status?
   - Drainage pattern favorable?
   - Patient factors (age, comorbidities)?

5. **Expected Outcomes:**
   - Low-flow lesions: >75% success with sclerotherapy
   - AVF: Mechanical occlusion effective
   - Complex AVM: May require multiple interventions
   - Chronic pain possible even with technical success
